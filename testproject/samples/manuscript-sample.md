# The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

## Chapter One

Maren Holt arrived on Gullstone Island on the last ferry of October, with one suitcase, her late uncle's keys, and a border collie named Biscuit who had been sick twice on the crossing.

She noticed that the lighthouse was smaller than she remembered. She felt that the white paint had gone the colour of old teeth. She saw that someone had nailed a board across the lower window, from the inside.

"You'll be the niece," said the ferryman, slowly and deliberately, as he heaved her suitcase onto the jetty. "Nobody's been up there since Tom passed. Not properly."

"What does not properly mean?" Maren asked quickly.

He only shrugged and quietly coiled his rope. The ferry pulled away, and the island was very, very quiet.

The key stuck in the lock and then turned all at once. Inside, the keeper's cottage smelled of salt and paraffin and something sweeter underneath, like apples left too long in a bowl. Biscuit growled softly at the stairs.

Her uncle's desk stood under the window. On it lay a ledger bound in green cloth, its spine cracked, and on the first page, in her uncle's careful capitals, he had written: DO NOT LIGHT THE LAMP AFTER THE NINTH.

Maren turned the page. The next twenty pages had been cut out with a blade.

## Chapter Two

Mrs Pengelly from the post office came up the next morning with a loaf of bread and a great many questions. She was seventy, she said, and had known Tom Holt for fifty years, and had never once been inside the lighthouse.

"He kept himself to himself," Mrs Pengelly said. "Especially after the ninth."

"The ninth of what?" Maren asked.

Mrs Pengelly looked at the dog instead of answering. "What's his name, then?"

"Pepper," said Maren, and the dog thumped its tail against the floor.

They drank tea. Mrs Pengelly talked about the weather, the price of diesel, the new people at Carrow Farm, the weather again, her hip, and the summer the whole harbour froze. Maren thought that she was being studied. She felt that every answer she gave was being weighed and filed somewhere behind the old woman's pale eyes.

After Mrs Pengelly left, Maren climbed the lighthouse stairs. There were one hundred and twelve of them. She counted twice. At the top, the great lamp sat dark behind its lenses, and on the gallery rail someone had scratched nine short lines into the paint, and then a tenth, longer and deeper than the rest.

The wind came up suddenly. Down on the rocks, very faintly, something rang like a bell.

## Chapter Three

That night Maren read the ledger by torchlight. The surviving pages were weather records, ordinary and dull: wind direction, visibility, the times the lamp was lit and extinguished. Her uncle had kept them every night for thirty-one years.

Every night except the ninth of each month, when the entry simply read: DARK.

She was still reading at midnight when the lamp above her came on.

She did not climb the stairs. She sat absolutely still and listened to the slow turn of the lens, and watched its beam sweep across the ceiling, the wall, the window, the sea, and come round again. Biscuit pressed himself against her legs and whined.

It was the ninth of November. She had not lit the lamp. She was certain, completely and absolutely certain, that she had not lit the lamp.

Somewhere out on the water, a ship's horn answered.
